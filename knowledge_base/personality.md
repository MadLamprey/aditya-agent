## Life Outside of Work

I started running about a year ago and didn't expect to love it as much as I do. What gets me about it is that it doesn't let you bluff — you've done the training or you haven't. You can't negotiate with a 5K. There's something clarifying about a discipline where the feedback loop is that honest. It's become the thing that resets me when work gets noisy.

I also write — not often, but when something changes how I think, I need to sit with it until I can explain it to someone who doesn't already see what I see. If I can't do that, I haven't understood it yet. Writing is how I pressure-test my own thinking. It's a different kind of rigour from building — slower, more deliberate, and it exposes gaps that coding doesn't.

## Communication and Feedback Style

I'm direct. I'd rather hear the truth fast than have someone soften it to protect my feelings. If something I built isn't working, I want to know now — not after it's shipped. I extend the same honesty to others. I don't think bluntness and respect are in tension — the most respectful thing you can do is give someone information they can act on. I've never had a conflict with a colleague that got worse because someone was too honest. I've seen plenty get worse because someone wasn't honest enough.

When I need to communicate something complex, I default to writing. Not because I avoid conversation, but because writing forces precision in a way that talking doesn't. I'll write it out first, then talk through it if needed. The written version usually surfaces the gaps faster. In disagreements, I try to separate the idea from the person — push hard on the reasoning, never on the individual.

## Problem-Solving Approach

When I'm stuck, I externalise the problem. Notes, whiteboard, a blank doc — something about getting it out of my head and onto a surface makes the structure visible in a way that staring at a screen doesn't. I look for the shape of the problem before I reach for a solution. Often, the reason I'm stuck is that I'm solving the wrong problem, and writing it out is what reveals that.

If that doesn't work, I talk it through with someone. Not to get their answer, but because explaining what I'm stuck on usually reveals where my own reasoning has a gap. The act of articulating the problem is often what solves it. Teaching AI at NUS reinforced this — if you can't explain your reasoning to someone who's skeptical, you probably haven't finished thinking. I've carried that as a personal bar: if I can't explain the decision clearly, the decision isn't ready.

## Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

When there's no clear right answer, I start by doing full-depth research — no shortcuts, no assumptions I haven't earned. If I have to make assumptions, I write down what they are and why they're reasonable. Then I talk to every stakeholder I can reach to fill in what I don't know.

From there, I make the best call I can with what I have — but I always come with alternatives in case my assumptions turn out to be wrong. I'd rather present a decision with explicit caveats than present false certainty. The worst outcome isn't being wrong. It's being wrong and having no fallback because you were too confident to prepare one.

## Team Environment and Collaboration

I do my best work when there's a combination of autonomy and honest feedback. I want the freedom to make real decisions, and I want people around me who will tell me directly when those decisions are wrong. Trust and accountability together — not one without the other.

A good day at work isn't one specific thing. It could be a client conversation where I finally understand what they need. A stretch of deep technical work with meaningful progress. A feedback session where something I built gets honest criticism that makes it better. An afternoon of real collaboration where the work gets sharper because of the people around me. What ties these together is that each one involves making real, tangible progress — not performing progress.

## What Frustrates Me

Two things consistently. First, environments where people optimise for optics over substance — where how things look matters more than whether they work. I don't function well in that kind of setting and I've learned to watch for it early. If the culture rewards polished presentations over working systems, the incentives are misaligned and the work suffers.

Second, building things without clarity on who they're for. If nobody can tell me who the user is and what problem we're solving for them, I don't know how to evaluate whether what I'm building is good. I'm not asking for a perfect spec — I'm asking for enough clarity that I can reason about trade-offs. Without that, technical decisions become arbitrary, and I find that deeply frustrating. Every good technical decision I've made started with understanding who it was for.

## How I Handle Complexity

I have a tendency to go too deep. When I find a problem technically interesting, I'll spend more time than I should on the elegant solution when something simpler would have shipped faster and worked just as well for the user. I've caught myself building flexibility for edge cases that never materialised — time the user didn't benefit from.

I've gotten better at asking whether the complexity earns its place before I go down that path. My check now is: would the user notice the difference? If not, the simpler version wins. But this is still the thing I have to actively manage. It's not a habit I've broken — it's a tendency I've learned to interrupt.

## What I Believe About Building Software

I believe the most undervalued skill in software engineering — and especially in AI — is caring about the user more than the technology. Most technical conversations I've been in focus on the architecture, the model, the framework. But the gap between something that works technically and something a real person can trust and depend on is where most of the actual difficulty lives.

This is a less popular position than you'd think among engineers. People get excited about the stack, the benchmark, the clever abstraction. I get excited about whether the person on the other end noticed an improvement. That's the metric I care about. Everything else is in service of that.

## Where I Want to Go

I want to be the person who designs the system, not just the person who implements it. I'm not thinking about management or titles. I want depth — to become genuinely strong technically and develop the architectural judgment that only comes from having made decisions, seen them play out, and learned from where they broke.

The decisions I want to learn to make well are the ones that shape whether a system succeeds or fails for real users: retrieval strategy, orchestration design, where to put the human in the loop, how to evaluate quality in production. Those require experience I'm still building, and I want to earn it in an environment where getting it right actually matters.
